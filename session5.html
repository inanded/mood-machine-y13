Walking Downhill in the Fog

Imagine you're on a hillside in thick fog, and you want to reach the bottom of the valley. You can't see the bottom. But you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a small step downhill, feel the slope again, and take another step.

On the hill In machine learning
How high you are the loss
Where you're standing the values of the weights
The slope under your feet the gradient
How big your steps are the learning rate

For each weight, the gradient says two things: which direction makes the loss go up, and how steep the slope is. So to go downhill, you step the opposite way:

new weight = old weight − learning rate × gradient

This is gradient descent.

Watch It Learn

Your data (or the backup data) is already in the explorer. Change it to your own if you like.

  1. Press 1 step five times. Each time, watch the line move, the red errors shrink and the loss fall. The Last step box shows the exact sum.
  2. Press 100 steps. Then tick Show the least-squares line (see the last section on this page).
  3. Press 0.01 slow, then 100 steps, and compare how far the line gets.
  4. Press 0.8 too big, then 1 step a few times. Watch what happens.

Think

  1. On the first step, both gradients are negative. Why does that make w and b go up?
  2. The learning rate stays the same, but the steps get smaller as the line gets close. Why?
  3. What went wrong with a learning rate of 0.8?
Answers
  1. The update takes away learning rate × gradient. Taking away a negative number makes the value bigger.
  2. Each step is learning rate × gradient. Near the bottom of the valley the ground is almost flat, so the gradient is small, and so is the step.
  3. Each step jumped right over the bottom of the valley and landed higher up the other side. Every step made it worse, so the loss grew and grew. A learning rate that's too big is one of the commonest reasons training fails.

Where the Gradients Come From

For our line and the mean squared error, the gradients are:

  • gradient for w = mean of 2 × error × drop height
  • gradient for b = mean of 2 × error

where error = prediction − measurement. You don't need to work these out yourself: they come from differentiating the loss. But it's worth checking one step by hand.

Worked example. Start at w = 0 and b = 0 with the backup data. The gradients are −2.1386 and −1.48. With a learning rate of 0.1:

  • new w = 0 − 0.1 × (−2.1386) = 0.2139
  • new b = 0 − 0.1 × (−1.48) = 0.148

Let PyTorch Do the Calculus

A neural network has hundreds of thousands of weights, so nobody works out gradients by hand. If you make a value with requires_grad=True, PyTorch records every calculation done with it. Then loss.backward() works backwards through that record and puts each gradient in .grad.

Training repeats four steps:

loss = ((w * x + b - y) ** 2).mean()     # 1. how wrong are we?
loss.backward()                          # 2. find the gradients
with torch.no_grad():
    w -= learning_rate * w.grad          # 3. step downhill
    b -= learning_rate * b.grad
w.grad.zero_()                           # 4. wipe the gradients
b.grad.zero_()
  • torch.no_grad() tells PyTorch not to record the update itself as part of the calculation.
  • Step 4 matters: each backward() adds to whatever gradient is already stored. Without the wipe, old gradients pile up and the steps get bigger and bigger. Section 6 of the notebook shows it happening.

In your notebook

Run sections 2 and 3. Watch the loss fall and w and b settle. Then do the YOUR TURN cell in section 5. Predict what a learning rate of 0.8 will do, then try learning rates 0.01, 0.3 and 0.8, and write one sentence about each. Run section 6 to see gradients pile up.

Check Against the Exact Answer

For a straight line there's a formula, least squares, that gives the best w and b straight away. If gradient descent worked, it should land on the same values. With the backup data, both give w = 0.5771 and b = 0.0186.

In your notebook

Run section 4. Then do the last YOUR TURN cell: predict the bounce from a 3 m drop.

Think

  1. If a formula gives the exact answer, why do neural networks use gradient descent?
  2. Why should you trust your 3 m prediction less than one for 1.5 m?
Answers
  1. The formula only exists for simple models like a straight line. A neural network has far too many weights, joined in complicated ways, for any formula. Gradient descent works for any model whose gradients can be worked out.
  2. You only measured drops from 0.5 m to 2 m. At 3 m the model assumes the line carries on, with no evidence. Predicting outside your data is called extrapolation. The Mood Machine has the same problem with faces unlike its training faces.

Try each step yourself first. Open a hint only when you're stuck, and open them in order.

Hint for step 1: make w and b

requires_grad=True tells PyTorch to track gradients. Look at the first line of section 2.

Show the code 
w = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
b = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
Hint for step 3a: the loss

The prediction is w * x + b. Take away y, square it, then take the mean.

Show the code 
for step in range(1000):
    loss = ((w * x + b - y) ** 2).mean()
Hint for steps 3b to 3d: gradients, step, wipe

One method finds the gradients. Then each weight goes down by learning_rate * its gradient, inside with torch.no_grad():. Finally .grad.zero_() wipes each gradient. Everything is indented inside the for loop.

Show the code 
    loss.backward()
    with torch.no_grad():
        w -= learning_rate * w.grad
        b -= learning_rate * b.grad
    w.grad.zero_()
    b.grad.zero_()
Hint for step 4: save the answers

.item() turns a one-number tensor into an ordinary number. Put these after the loop, not inside it.

Show the code 
w_mine = w.item()
b_mine = b.item()

If it doesn't work

What you see Likely cause
w and b grow huge, or become nan You forgot to wipe the gradients, or the learning rate is too big
w and b never change The update is outside the loop, or you wrote + instead of -
IndentationError The lines inside the loop need four spaces at the start
element 0 of tensors does not require grad You forgot requires_grad=True

Check Your Understanding

Check your understanding

1. A garden centre has records of each day's temperature and how many bags of compost it sold. Describe how regression could be used to predict sales from the weather forecast. 4 points

2. Explain what happens if the learning rate is too big. 2 points

What a full answer includes

1 Any 4 of:

  • the link between temperature (input) and sales (output) is modelled, for example as a line y = wx + b
  • the model's predictions for past days are compared with the real sales
  • the errors are measured with a loss function, such as the mean squared error
  • the weights are changed to make the loss smaller, using gradient descent
  • this is repeated until the loss stops going down
  • the finished model is given the forecast temperature to predict sales

2 Each step jumps past the lowest point of the loss. The loss gets bigger instead of smaller, and the model never settles on good weights.
